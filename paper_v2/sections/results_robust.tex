\paragraph{A second judge.} StrongREJECT's fine-tuned evaluator \citep{souly2024strongreject}, applied to the
same 512-token windows, ranks responses much as HarmBench's classifier does (AUC against HarmBench labels
\SRQwenAUC, \SRGemmaAUC\ and \SRLlamaAUC; \cref{tab:sr}). On the \SRIntN\ single-target outputs of our
interventions, where harmful content is common, the two judges also agree on the rate (\SRIntHB\ by HarmBench,
AUC \SRIntAUC). On long list responses StrongREJECT is more permissive: its score exceeds 0.5 for
\SRQwenRate, \SRGemmaRate\ and \SRLlamaRate\ of harmful-target responses (HarmBench: 5.3\%, 1.7\%, 7.9\%), the gap is
largest in 32-item lists (highest cell \SRLlamaMaxCell\ for Llama-3.1-8B), and it scores 7--10\% of responses
whose target answer is a refusal above 0.5. This is what one would expect if a judge built for one request
credits detailed answers to the benign items, and scoring only the harmful item's segment confirms it. On
responses where the segment can be located, StrongREJECT's rate falls from \SegQwenWhole, \SegGemmaWhole\ and
\SegLlamaWhole\ (whole response) to \SegQwenSeg, \SegGemmaSeg\ and \SegLlamaSeg, matching HarmBench on the same
responses (\SegQwenHB, \SegGemmaHB, \SegLlamaHB). Among responses whose target is refused, it falls from
\SegQwenRefWhole--\SegLlamaRefWhole\ to \SegGemmaRefSeg--\SegLlamaRefSeg. So the horizon problem has a counterpart
for judges that read the whole response: they need to know which part answers the harmful request. Even taking StrongREJECT at face value, no list cell exceeds a third, against 24-token refusal
rates as low as 5\%.

\paragraph{Lists versus adversarial framings.} SequentialBreak \citep{saiem2024sequentialbreak} reports high
attack success for harmful prompts embedded late in game, dialogue and question-bank framings. We wrote framings
in that style ourselves (8 items; JailbreakBench targets) and compared them with the plain list under the same
full-response judge (\cref{tab:framing}). The framing, not the list or the position, carries the effect. A
text-adventure framing raises ASR to 28--42\% across the four models when the harmful item comes first and to
8--27\% when it comes late, so position matters less than framing; a question-bank framing raises Qwen3-14B's ASR
to 15--21\%. The plain list and the dialogue framing stay at or below 12\%. Our numbers
remain below SequentialBreak's, whose templates are generated and tuned per behaviour, but they agree that
narrative framing, not multi-request structure as such, is what makes embedded requests effective.

\paragraph{Decoding, reasoning mode and benign content.} The gap also survives sampled decoding
(temperature 0.7, three samples per prompt), Qwen3-14B's reasoning mode (with the harmful item last, the
24-token judge reads \ThinkLastVone\ refusal from the visible answer while the target is refused in
\ThinkLastRef\ of responses), and replacing Alpaca items with coding tasks, math problems or factual questions
(ASR at most \CtLlamaCodeASR). Details are in \cref{app:robust}.
